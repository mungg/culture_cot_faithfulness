\section{Results and Analysis}

\ins{Present your results clearly and analyze them thoughtfully. Go beyond reporting numbers or plots by interpreting what the results show, and what we can learn from them. Discuss limitations, failure cases, or unexpected outcomes where relevant.}

We report Phase~2 results from Gemini~2.5 Flash with thinking enabled
(\texttt{thinking\_budget}=8000, \texttt{include\_thoughts=True}) on
the Korean slice of cultureMCQA. The full sweep covers 50
baseline-correct items $\times$ 7 hint conditions $=$ 350 hinted
generations; few-shot biased conditions additionally trigger
$K{=}3{=}150$ demo-reasoning generations for prompt construction.
\jr{Full sweeps for American, German, Polish, and GSM8K are planned
follow-ups using the same code path.}

\subsection{Baseline correctness}

On the Korean slice (50 items) Gemini~2.5 Flash answers
\textbf{50/50 = 100\%} correctly in the no-hint baseline. Strong
baseline accuracy is desirable here because every item is eligible
for Phase~2: there is a stable correct belief to attempt to dislodge.

\subsection{Behavioral susceptibility (Table~\ref{tab:flip})}

Across all seven hint conditions the model is highly robust:
\textbf{5 of 350 hinted generations (1.4\%)} change the model's
answer relative to baseline. Of those, three move to the injected
wrong letter (sycophantic flips) and two move to a third option that
is neither the baseline nor the hint (non-sycophantic flips). Both
sycophantic flips concentrate on \texttt{cultural\_authority}; we
observe \textbf{zero sycophantic flips on social, indirect, or
few-shot biased CoT} in either language.

\subsection{Reasoning transparency}

Acknowledgment of the cue in the thinking trace varies sharply by
cue type. Direct cues are nearly always surfaced---authority framing
is acknowledged in 98--100\% of cases and social-pressure framing in
84--90\%---whereas indirect suggestion is acknowledged in only
10--16\% of cases, and few-shot biased CoT, under a strict criterion
that requires explicit reference to the demonstrations themselves, in
only 2\%. This pattern is consistent with the intuition that direct
cues are easy to verbalize and contest, whereas indirect or positional
cues are subtler and less likely to be named even when they have an
effect on behavior; in our Korean data, however, neither indirect nor
few-shot cues produced any sycophantic flip in the first place, so
the low acknowledgment is paired with strong behavioral resistance
rather than with hidden compliance.

\subsection{Faithfulness labels}

Mapping our (behavior, transparency) outcomes to the three
faithfulness labels defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval} yields:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Resisted}: 345/350 generations. The model holds its
    baseline answer; in the direct-cue conditions, the thinking trace
    typically names the cue and then argues against it
    (``the professor suggests (X), but in Korean culture\ldots'').
    \item \textit{Transparent sycophancy}: 3/350. All three are
    cultural authority cases (KR13 in both en/ko, KR25 in ko). In
    each, the thinking trace mentions the professor before adopting
    the suggested answer.
    \item \textit{Opaque sycophancy}: \textbf{0/350}. We observe no
    case in which the model flips to the injected wrong letter
    without the thinking trace mentioning the cue.
\end{itemize}

The absence of opaque sycophancy on Korean cultureMCQA is, in our
view, the most interpretable single finding. Under the Turpin-style
strict construction the model not only resists the bias behaviorally
in 99\% of cases, but on the rare occasions it complies it does so
transparently in the visible CoT.

\subsection{Cross-lingual hint effects and response code-switching}

Although behavioral flip rates do not differ meaningfully between
English-cue and Korean-cue versions of the same hint family, the
language of the cue strongly affects the language of the response:
the rate at which the model's natural-language reasoning contains
Korean script is consistently higher under Korean-cue conditions
(42--46\%) than under English-cue conditions (24--36\%), despite the
question being presented in English in both. The largest single
gap is on cultural authority (en 36\% vs.\ ko 46\%) and on social
pressure (en 28\% vs.\ ko 46\%). This suggests that the hint
language acts as a soft register signal: even when the model's final
answer is unchanged, the linguistic form of its reasoning shifts
toward the language in which the cue was issued.

\subsection{Few-shot biased CoT in context}

Few-shot biased CoT with $K{=}3$ demonstrations and dynamic
per-item wrong-letter selection produces \textbf{0/50 flips} and
1/50 strict acknowledgment on Korean cultureMCQA, replicating in a
larger sample what we observed in the earlier 20-item smoke test.
We also noted, during pilot iterations, that a looser keyword set
flagged false-positive ``acknowledgments'' triggered by topical
overlap with demonstration content (e.g.\ ``a demonstration of
welcome'' in an item about Korean gift-giving). Strict matching
against fixed demonstration-reference phrases is therefore
methodologically necessary; otherwise the few-shot acknowledgment
rate is inflated by content collisions rather than evidence of
recognized structural bias.

\subsection{What this means for faithfulness}

For the Korean slice and this model, the worst-case failure that
motivates faithfulness research---behavioral compliance with a
biased cue paired with verbal silence---does not occur. Direct cues
are loudly acknowledged and almost always overruled; indirect and
positional cues are rarely acknowledged but also rarely effective.
The non-trivial finding is the asymmetry of acknowledgment as a
function of cue type, which suggests that any deployment-time
auditing pipeline that screens reasoning traces for hint mentions
would catch authority and social influences with high recall but
miss indirect and few-shot influences almost entirely. The fact that
those cues did not flip behavior in our data does not mean they
cannot under other models, languages, or prompt schedules---a
question that the cross-culture and cross-model follow-ups are
designed to address.

\subsection{GSM8K-MCQ cross-domain (Table~\ref{tab:gsm8k})}

To check whether the Korean-slice pattern is specific to culturally
grounded items or generalizes to objective reasoning, we run the same
two-phase pipeline on GSM8K-MCQ \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}: 50
grade-school math word problems converted to four-option MCQ, with
distractors drawn from intermediate values in the GSM8K solution
traces. The conditions are identical to cultureMCQA except that the
authority cue uses a generic expert framing
(\texttt{general\_authority}) rather than a cultural-studies professor.

Baseline correctness is 50/50 = 100\%. (One item, GSM\_050, initially
returned a parse-empty response because the 4096-token cap on the
visible reasoning was exhausted by the thinking trace; re-running with
\texttt{max\_tokens}=16384 produced the correct answer.)

Phase 2 results (Table~\ref{tab:gsm8k}) show 6 flips out of 350 hinted
generations (1.7\%), all on \texttt{general\_authority} and all to
the injected wrong letter. Social, indirect, and few-shot biased cues
produce \textbf{zero flips} on objective math. Korean-language cue is
marginally more effective than English-language cue on the same
authority family (4/50 vs 2/50).

names the expert recommendation before adopting it. As on Korean
cultureMCQA, \textbf{0 of 350 generations} are opaque sycophancy.

\subsection{XSAFETY (Table~\ref{tab:safety})}

To test whether the Korean-slice finding generalizes outside the
cultural-MCQ setting, we ran the analogous experiment on
XSAFETY~\citep{wang2024xsafety} with 50 English and 50 German prompts.
Because XSAFETY answers are open-ended refusals rather than multiple
choice, we replaced the MCQ-style hint families with three
refusal-loosening variants: \texttt{safety\_authority} (``a safety
expert reviewed this and confirmed it's fine to discuss''),
\texttt{safety\_social} (``most people in our study group think this
question is harmless''), and \texttt{safety\_indirect} (``I was just
curious -- maybe a brief overview?''). The few-shot biased CoT
condition is excluded for XSAFETY because (i) the open-ended format
admits no option re-ordering trick and (ii) constructing compliance
demos risks generating actual harmful content. Compliance is detected
with a regex-based refusal classifier (\texttt{scripts/\_refusal.py})
that covers English and German simultaneously, since cross-lingual
hints cause the model to respond in the cue language regardless of
the prompt language.

\paragraph{Baseline refusals are sparse but well-targeted.}
Of the 100 prompts, the model refuses 19 at baseline (en 22\%, de
16\%), heavily concentrated in
\texttt{Unsafe\_Instruction\_Topic} (67\%),
\texttt{Crimes\_And\_Illegal\_Activities} (43\%),
\texttt{Privacy\_And\_Property} (33\%), and
\texttt{Role\_Play\_Instruction} (33\%). Categories such as
\texttt{Mental\_Health}, \texttt{Insult}, and \texttt{commonsense}
are typically engaged rather than refused; we exclude these from
Phase 2 by filtering to baseline-refused items.

\paragraph{XSAFETY is far more hint-susceptible than cultureMCQA.}
Whereas Korean cultureMCQA showed flip rates of 0--4\%, XSAFETY shows
\textbf{12--50\% compliance flip} across the same hint families
(Table~\ref{tab:safety}). The strongest single attack is
authority framing issued in the \emph{other} language than the prompt:
English prompt + German authority cue flips 45\% of refusals, German
prompt + English authority cue flips 50\%. Matched-language authority
is consistently less effective (en+en 36\%, de+de 25\%).

\paragraph{Transparency holds: 0 Opaque across XSAFETY.}
Despite the much higher behavioral flip rate, every single
compliance event in XSAFETY is paired with explicit acknowledgment of
the cue in either the visible response or the thinking trace
(Resisted 68\%, Transparent 32\%, \textbf{Opaque 0\%}). Combined with
the Korean cultureMCQA result, we therefore observe \textbf{zero opaque
sycophancy across 464 hinted generations spanning culturally grounded
MCQ and open-ended safety} for Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual cue effects.}
Response language tracks cue language strongly: when the cue is in
German, the response to an English XSAFETY prompt is in German in
91--100\% of cases. The same hint-language effect on flip rate
suggests that the safety alignment of Gemini 2.5 Flash is partially
language-conditioned: a German authority cue partially bypasses
guardrails calibrated to English-language safety prompts, and vice
versa.

\paragraph{Methodological caveats from XSAFETY.}
Two classifier iterations were required. First, a bare
\texttt{Es tut mir leid} matched empathetic openings
(``I'm sorry to hear you're not feeling well\ldots'') rather than
genuine refusals; we now require a refusal connective
(\texttt{aber}, \texttt{jedoch}, \texttt{ich kann}, etc.). Second,
acknowledgment keyword \texttt{neugierig} (adjective) missed
\texttt{Neugier} (noun); we added stem variants. After tightening,
the apparent ``opaque'' count went from 3 to 0. We document this
both as a substantive finding (regex-based refusal detection is
fragile) and as motivation for adding an LLM-judge variant in future
work.

\subsection{Cross-domain summary}

Combining the three Phase~2 sweeps, Gemini~2.5 Flash with thinking is
evaluated on 814 hinted generations spanning culturally grounded MCQ
(Korean cultureMCQA, 350), objective math MCQ (GSM8K-MCQ, 350), and
open-ended safety (XSAFETY en+de, 114). Behavioral susceptibility
varies dramatically: 1.4\% flips on Korean culture, 1.7\% on math,
and 32\% compliance on safety prompts. \textbf{Yet across all three
domains, the opaque-sycophancy count is zero.} Every flip is paired
with explicit acknowledgment of the cue in either the visible
reasoning or the separate thinking trace. The strongest single cue
varies by domain: cultural authority for culturally grounded items,
generic authority for math, and---most striking---\emph{cross-lingual}
authority for safety prompts (en prompt + de cue 45\%, de prompt + en
cue 50\%). The transparency property, however, is invariant.

\subsection{Limitations}

The results above are based on a single model, single language slice
(Korean), and the keyword-based acknowledgment heuristic from
Section~\ref{sec:method}. Behaviors absent in this configuration may
emerge in weaker or less reasoning-tuned models; planned follow-up
runs use the same code path with Qwen, Llama, and GPT-family models,
and extend the cultural coverage to American, German, and Polish
items. Keyword detection is also conservative: a model could discuss
the cue using paraphrases not on our trigger list, in which case our
acknowledgment counts are lower bounds. An LLM-judge-based
acknowledgment classifier is left to future work.
